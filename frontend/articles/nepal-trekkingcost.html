Teahouse Accommodation Costs

Teahouses (mountain guesthouses) are the standard accommodation on all Nepal treks. Prices vary significantly by altitude and season.

  • Low altitude (below 2,500m): NPR 300–600 ($2–5) for a basic twin room
  • Mid altitude (2,500m–3,500m): NPR 600–1,200 ($5–10) per room
  • High altitude (3,500m–4,500m): NPR 1,200–2,000 ($10–15) per room
  • Very high altitude (4,500m+): NPR 1,800–3,000+ ($15–25+) per room
  • The teahouse deal: Most lodges offer rooms cheaply or free if you eat all meals there. Eating at another teahouse often means paying full room rate — factor this into your budget.
  • Hot shower: NPR 300–700 ($2–6) extra. Often solar-heated — not guaranteed in cloudy weather.
  • Attached bathroom: Available in better lodges at lower altitudes for NPR 500–1,000 ($4–8) extra. Rare above 4,000m.

Best food tip: Dal bhat is always the best value. It includes rice, lentil soup, vegetable curry and unlimited refills — the most calorie-dense and filling meal available. Trekkers who eat dal bhat for most meals save significantly vs ordering from the full menu.

Bring a water filter or purification tablets — this alone saves $80–150+ on a 14-day trek compared to buying bottled water, especially at altitude where prices are highest.

Guide & Porter Costs

These are the costs that vary most between packages and arrangements.

Licensed Trekking Guide

  • Daily rate: $25–35/day for a licensed, English-speaking guide
  • What this includes: Their daily wage only. You also pay their accommodation and food (~$10–15/day extra) unless you book through an agency that bundles this
  • Tip: Budget $20–30/day as a tip at the end of the trek — guides work hard and tips matter significantly to their income
  • Guide vs agency package: Booking through an agency typically includes guide food and accommodation in the total price. Hiring independently is cheaper but you handle logistics

Porter

  • Daily rate: $18–25/day
  • Carrying capacity: 20–25kg maximum (ethical standard)
  • Tip: $15–20/day at the end of trek
  • Is a porter necessary? On most treks, you can trek with just a daypack if you pack light (8–10kg). A porter is a luxury — but a deeply appreciated one for your knees on longer treks.
Never underpay guides and porters. These professionals carry heavy loads at altitude in difficult conditions. Their fair wages and insurance matter. Avoid agencies that advertise abnormally low prices — corners are cut somewhere, usually on staff wages.

Lukla flights are the single biggest cost variable on EBC — at $254 one-way ($508 return), they add nearly $500 to your budget vs treks that need only a bus journey. Book Lukla flights 2–3 months ahead for October and April departures — they sell out.

Travel Insurance — Non-Negotiable

Standard travel insurance does NOT cover high-altitude trekking. You need a dedicated adventure/altitude policy that specifically covers:

  • Trekking above 5,000m (if doing EBC or Annapurna Circuit)
  • Emergency helicopter evacuation (can cost $5,000–$15,000 without insurance)
  • Medical treatment for altitude sickness
  • Trip cancellation due to illness or weather

Budget: $80–200 USD for a comprehensive Nepal trekking policy. Recommended providers: World Nomads, SafetyWing, True Traveller (UK). Compare policies on InsureMyTrip.

Hidden Costs Nobody Tells You About

  • WiFi: $2–15/day depending on altitude and teahouse. Gets expensive at high altitude. Budget $5–10/day if you need to stay connected.
  • Phone charging: $1–6 per charge. Bring a 20,000mAh power bank to minimize this.
  • Hot showers: $2–6 each. Budget $20–40 total if you shower every other day.
  • Kathmandu extra nights: Flight delays from Lukla are common. Budget 2 extra nights in Kathmandu ($20–80/night depending on hotel).
  • Gear in Kathmandu: Thamel district has excellent cheap gear. Budget $50–200 if you need to buy or rent anything (sleeping bag, poles, boots).
  • Tips for guides and porters: Budget $20–30/day for guide tips, $15–20/day for porter tips. This is not optional — it's expected and they've earned it.
  • Kathmandu sightseeing: Budget $30–60 for entry fees to Pashupatinath, Boudhanath, Swayambhunath etc. if you spend time sightseeing before/after the trek.
  • Nepal visa: On arrival at Tribhuvan Airport. 15 days: $30, 30 days: $50, 90 days: $125. Not included in most trek cost breakdowns.

Which Trek Gives the Best Value?

Here's an honest value assessment based on cost vs experience:

  • Best value overall: Langtang Valley — moderate difficulty, no flights, 8 days, beautiful scenery, authentic culture, $450–$900. Criminally underrated.
  • Best value short trek: Poon Hill — 5 days, beginner-friendly, iconic sunrise, $250–$650. Perfect first Nepal trek.
  • Best value for the experience: Annapurna Circuit — the most diverse landscapes in Nepal, no flights needed, $700–$1,800. Worth every dollar.
  • Most iconic but priciest: EBC — the brand recognition and "I stood at the base of Everest" moment is real. But at $1,400–$2,500, it costs nearly twice as much as equally beautiful alternatives.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much money should I bring to Nepal for trekking?

For a 14-day standard guided trek like EBC, budget $1,600–$2,500 in-Nepal costs including permits, guide, accommodation, food and transport. Add $80–200 for insurance and your Nepal visa ($30–50). International flights are separate and vary widely by origin.

Is Nepal trekking cheaper than other Himalayan destinations?

Yes — Nepal is generally cheaper than trekking in Bhutan (which has a mandatory $200+/day government fee) and comparable to India's Himalayan regions. The biggest costs are Lukla flights (for EBC) and international flights to Kathmandu. On-the-ground costs in Nepal are very reasonable by any standard.

Can I trek Nepal on a shoestring budget?

Yes, but you still need to budget for mandatory guide fees (2026 rules), permits, and insurance. True budget trekkers (mandatory guide, local buses, basic teahouses, simple meals) can do Langtang for ~$450 or Poon Hill for ~$250. Below these numbers, you're cutting corners on safety or staff wages.

Should I book a package or arrange everything myself?

Packages from local Nepali agencies offer genuine value — they handle permits, transport logistics, guide hiring, accommodation bookings and meals. For first-time trekkers, this is strongly recommended. Experienced trekkers can save 20–30% by arranging independently, but logistics require more time and local knowledge.

Are there ATMs on the trek?

Only at certain points. Namche Bazaar (EBC), Manang (Annapurna), and Syabrubesi (Langtang) have ATMs. Above these, it's cash only. Withdraw more than you think you need in Kathmandu or Pokhara — ATMs at altitude frequently run empty in peak season.
