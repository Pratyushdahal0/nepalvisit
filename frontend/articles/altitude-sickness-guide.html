Altitude sickness kills trekkers in Nepal every year — not because it's unpreventable, but because people ignore the warning signs or push through when they shouldn't. This guide covers everything: what's happening in your body, how to spot it early, and exactly what to do. Read it before you leave for the trailhead.

The One Rule That Saves Lives

Never ascend with symptoms. If symptoms worsen — descend immediately. Not tomorrow. Not after one more night. Immediately. This single rule, followed correctly, prevents almost every altitude-related death.

What Is Altitude Sickness?

Altitude sickness — medically called Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS) — happens when your body ascends faster than it can adapt to the lower oxygen levels at high elevation. The higher you go, the less oxygen is in each breath. Above 3,500m, every breath delivers roughly 40% less oxygen than at sea level.

Your body needs time to compensate — producing more red blood cells, adjusting breathing patterns, changing blood chemistry. When you climb faster than this adaptation process, your body falls behind. That's AMS.

The "Zone of Tolerance" Concept

At any point during a trek, your body is acclimatized to roughly the altitude where you last slept. Moving above that creates a grey zone where your body can function but is under strain. Move too far above it too fast, and AMS kicks in. The zone shifts upward as you acclimatize — which is why rest days and the "climb high, sleep low" principle work.

Who Gets Altitude Sickness?

This is the part most trekkers get wrong. Fitness has almost nothing to do with it.

Fitness Does Not Protect You

Marathon runners, elite cyclists and experienced mountaineers get AMS. Meanwhile some first-time trekkers sail through to 5,000m with no symptoms. The determining factors are genetics, your rate of ascent, and how well you acclimatize — not how fit you are. Never let fitness give you false confidence above 3,500m.

What does increase your risk:

Risk Factor Why It Matters
Previous AMS history If you've had it before, you're more likely to get it again at similar altitudes
Fast ascent rate Gaining too much altitude too quickly is the #1 cause
Flying straight to high altitude Flying to Lukla (2,860m) from sea level with no acclimatization is a common mistake
Dehydration Dehydration makes AMS significantly worse and faster to develop
Alcohol consumption Impairs breathing during sleep — exactly when your body acclimatizes most
Sleeping pills / sedatives Suppress breathing at night, reducing acclimatization

The 3 Types of Altitude Illness

Altitude illness comes in three forms, ranging from manageable to life-threatening. Understanding all three is non-negotiable before any high-altitude trek.

AMS

Acute Mountain Sickness

The most common form. Affects 30–40% of EBC trekkers at some point. Manageable if caught early and treated correctly — dangerous if ignored.

Manageable

HACE

High Altitude Cerebral Oedema

Brain swelling. What happens when AMS becomes a neurological emergency. Can kill within hours if untreated. Requires immediate descent.

Life-threatening

HAPE

High Altitude Pulmonary Oedema

Fluid in the lungs. The leading cause of altitude-related death in Nepal. Can develop rapidly and without obvious AMS warning signs first.

Life-threatening

Symptoms by Severity

Know these cold. The difference between mild AMS and a life-threatening emergency is how quickly you identify and respond.

Mild AMS — Stop and Rest

Symptom What to Do
HeadacheRest, hydrate, do not ascend further
Fatigue / weaknessRest at current altitude
Nausea (without vomiting)Eat lightly, stay hydrated
Dizziness / lightheadednessSit or lie down, rest
Poor sleep / insomniaNormal at altitude — rest anyway
Loss of appetiteForce fluids, eat carbs if possible

Moderate AMS — Consider Descending

Symptom What to Do
Severe, persistent headache not responding to ibuprofenDo not ascend — consider descent
VomitingDescend 300–500m, monitor closely
Extreme fatigue, can't walk normallyDescend immediately
Shortness of breath at restDescend immediately
HACE Symptoms — Descend Immediately, No Exceptions

Confusion or disorientation · Loss of coordination (can't walk straight) · Slurred speech · Severe disorientation · Loss of consciousness. Do not wait until morning. Descend now, even in darkness if necessary. Give dexamethasone if available. Get to a lower altitude — minimum 300–1,000m descent.

HAPE Symptoms — Medical Emergency, Descend Immediately

Breathlessness at rest · Persistent dry cough, later producing pink or frothy sputum · Extreme fatigue · Blue tinge to lips or fingernails (cyanosis) · Gurgling or crackling sound in chest. HAPE can develop without prior AMS warning. This is the leading cause of altitude death in Nepal. Descend immediately and get emergency help.

The Golden Rule of Altitude

Climb High, Sleep Low

During the day, you can ascend to a higher altitude for acclimatization. But always return to a lower elevation to sleep. Your body does most of its acclimatization work while you sleep — make sure it's doing that work at a manageable altitude.

The practical rule for ascent above 3,000m: don't gain more than 300–500m of sleeping altitude per day. Every 3 days, take a full rest day at the same altitude before pushing higher.

On the EBC route this is built into the standard itinerary — the Namche rest day, the Dingboche rest day. Do not skip these rest days even if you feel completely fine. Feeling good at 3,500m gives no guarantee of how you'll feel at 4,500m.

The road access does not make the altitude any lower. Trekkers who skip stages are arriving at high altitude under-acclimatized.

Prevention — What Actually Works

Ascend Slowly — This Is the Only Reliable Prevention

No supplement, drug or fitness level substitutes for a slow ascent. Above 3,000m, limit sleeping altitude gain to 300–500m per day. Take rest days every 3 days.

Drink 3–4 Litres of Water Per Day

Dehydration compounds AMS dramatically. Drink before you feel thirsty. Bring a water filter or purification tablets — don't rely on buying bottled water at teahouses above Namche.

Skip Alcohol for the First Several Days

Alcohol suppresses breathing during sleep — the exact time your body does most acclimatization. The celebration beer can wait until you're back in Namche on the way down.

Avoid Sleeping Pills

Even mild sedatives suppress breathing at night. Insomnia at altitude is completely normal and not dangerous. Accept it — rest horizontally and let your body breathe naturally.

Eat Carbohydrate-Rich Foods

Your body metabolizes carbohydrates more efficiently in low-oxygen environments than proteins or fats. Dal bhat, noodle soup, rice and boiled potatoes aren't just comfort food — they're physiologically smart at altitude.

Spend a Night at Moderate Altitude Before Starting

If possible, spend a night or two above 2,000m before your trek begins. Flying direct from London or New York to Lukla (2,860m) and starting immediately is not ideal. A night in Kathmandu (1,400m) helps mildly — a night in Nagarkot (2,195m) is better.

Get Proper Trekking Insurance

Helicopter evacuation from altitude costs $3,000–$15,000 USD without insurance. Since 2026, insurance covering high-altitude evacuation to at least 6,000m has been strongly recommended (and increasingly required for permit issuance on some routes). Don't skip this.

Diamox — Does It Work?

Diamox (Acetazolamide) is the most commonly used medication for altitude sickness prevention and treatment. Here's what you actually need to know:

Question Answer
Does it work? Yes — it stimulates breathing and speeds up acclimatization by helping kidneys excrete bicarbonate
Preventive dose 125–250mg twice daily, starting 1–2 days before ascending above 2,500m
Treatment dose 250mg twice daily once symptoms appear
Side effects Increased urination, tingling in hands/feet, carbonated drinks taste flat — all normal and harmless
Who shouldn't take it People allergic to sulfa drugs. Check with a doctor before your trek.
Does it mask symptoms? No — it genuinely speeds acclimatization, not just masks symptoms. But it's not a substitute for ascending slowly.

Other Medications

Drug Use Notes
Ibuprofen Altitude headache relief Effective for headache. 400–600mg. Take with food.
Dexamethasone Emergency treatment for HACE/HAPE 8mg initial dose, then 4mg every 6 hours. Not for prevention — emergency only. Guides carry this.
Nifedipine Emergency treatment for HAPE Reduces pulmonary pressure. Carry on high-altitude routes if advised by your doctor.
See a doctor before your trek

Discuss Diamox with a doctor before leaving home — ideally a travel medicine specialist. They can prescribe the right dose, check for sulfa allergies, and advise on dexamethasone for your emergency kit. Most trekking agencies in Kathmandu can also advise on medication.

Treatment & When to Descend

For Mild AMS

1
Stop ascending immediately

Do not gain any more altitude. Stay at your current elevation.

2
Rest and hydrate

Drink 3–4 litres of water. Rest completely. Eat lightly if you can.

3
Take ibuprofen for headache

400–600mg with food. This manages the symptom but monitor whether the underlying AMS is improving.

4
Monitor for 12–24 hours

If symptoms improve fully — cautiously continue. If symptoms stay the same or worsen — descend 300–500m immediately.

For Moderate/Severe AMS, HACE or HAPE

Descend immediately — minimum 300–1,000m

Don't wait for morning. Don't wait to see if it improves. Get the person moving downhill now. Administer dexamethasone (8mg) if available. Call for helicopter evacuation if the person cannot walk. Even a descent of 300m can make a dramatic difference — get lower as fast as safely possible.

Helicopter Evacuation

If the trekker cannot walk or symptoms are severe, call for helicopter evacuation. Your trekking agency, guide, or the nearest teahouse can help contact rescue services. This is why insurance is essential — costs run $3,000–$15,000 USD depending on location and conditions.
