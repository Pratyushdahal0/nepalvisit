Overview

The Everest Base Camp trek is one of the most iconic journeys on earth. At 5,364 metres, base camp sits in the shadow of the world's highest peak — and getting there is a 14-day walk through Sherpa villages, Buddhist monasteries, glacial moraines and some of the most dramatic mountain scenery anywhere on the planet.

This guide is built on real 2026 data — updated permit fees, actual teahouse prices, current Lukla flight costs, and honest difficulty advice. No sponsored content, no inflated package prices.

2026 Update: Kathmandu–Lukla flights have increased to approximately $254 one-way ($508 return) due to aviation fuel surcharges — a 10–20% rise from 2024. Teahouse meal prices above Namche have risen 15–20% compared to 2023. Budget accordingly.

Is EBC Right for You?

EBC is hard — not because it requires technical climbing (it doesn't), but because of the cumulative effect of 14 days at altitude, walking 6–8 hours daily with a pack. The honest fitness benchmark: you should be able to hike uphill for 6 hours on consecutive days before you go. If you can do that, you can do EBC with proper preparation.

The main reason people fail to reach base camp is altitude sickness — not fitness. Read the altitude section below carefully. It's the most important thing in this guide.

Permits & Fees 2026

You need two permits for the EBC trek. Both can be obtained at the Nepal Tourism Board (NTB) office in Kathmandu's Bhrikutimandap area, or at checkpoints on the trail (though getting them in Kathmandu is faster).

Sagarmatha National Park Entry

NPR 3,000 ~$22 USD

Mandatory for all international trekkers. Checked at Monjo checkpoint. Buy in Kathmandu or at the park gate.

Khumbu Pasang Lhamu Rural Municipality

NPR 2,000 ~$15 USD

Local municipality fee introduced in 2021. Collected at Lukla or Monjo. Replaces the old TIMS card in this region.

Total permit cost: ~NPR 5,000 ($37–52 USD) — considerably less than most people expect. Note: SAARC nationals (India, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, etc.) pay reduced rates. Children under 10 are usually free.

Hidden Costs Nobody Tells You About

  • WiFi: $2–5/hour or $5–15/day pass at teahouses. Gets more expensive the higher you go.
  • Phone charging: $1–3 per charge at lower altitudes, up to $6 near Gorak Shep.
  • Hot showers: $2–6 per shower. Not always available above Dingboche.
  • Bottled water: $1 near Lukla, up to $4–6 per litre near Gorak Shep. Bring a water filter — saves $100+ and reduces plastic waste.
  • ATMs: The last reliable ATM is in Namche Bazaar. Withdraw enough cash before going higher. No card payments above Namche.
  • Guide/porter tips: Budget $20–30/day for your guide and $15–20/day for your porter as a tip at the end.

Altitude Sickness — The Most Important Section

Altitude sickness (AMS — Acute Mountain Sickness) affects roughly 50% of EBC trekkers to some degree. It is the number one reason people turn back before reaching base camp. Understanding it is more important than any other preparation you can do.

Symptoms — Know These Before You Go

  • Mild AMS (normal, manageable): Headache, fatigue, loss of appetite, difficulty sleeping. Rest, hydrate, don't ascend further until symptoms clear.
  • Moderate AMS (serious warning): Severe headache that doesn't respond to paracetamol, nausea, vomiting, dizziness. Descend 300–500m immediately.
  • HACE (brain swelling — emergency): Confusion, loss of coordination, can't walk straight. Descend immediately, use supplemental oxygen if available, evacuate.
  • HAPE (lung fluid — emergency): Breathlessness at rest, wet cough, blue lips. Descend immediately. This is life-threatening within hours if untreated.

Prevention

  • Never ascend more than 300–500m per day above 3,000m (the standard rule)
  • The acclimatization days at Namche and Dingboche are medically designed — never skip them
  • Drink 3–4 litres of water per day — dehydration makes AMS worse
  • No alcohol above 3,500m
  • Consider Diamox (acetazolamide) — ask your doctor at least 2 weeks before travel
  • Bring a pulse oximeter — monitors your blood oxygen levels daily (buy one for ~$20)
The golden rule: If in doubt, go down. Helicopter evacuation from Gorak Shep costs $5,000–$15,000 without insurance. HACE and HAPE develop quickly and kill within hours. No view or itinerary is worth your life. Descend first, reassess later.

Best Teahouses on the Route

Teahouses range from $5–10/night for basic twin rooms to $25+ at higher altitudes. The unwritten rule: most teahouses offer cheap or free rooms if you eat all your meals there. Eating elsewhere can result in paying full room rate.

Namche Bazaar 3,440m
Yeti Mountain Home / Khumbu Lodge

Hot showers, reliable WiFi, heated dining room. Namche has the best facilities on the entire route. Book ahead for October and April.

Tengboche 3,860m
Rivendell Lodge / Tengboche Guest House

Incredible Ama Dablam and Everest views from the dining room. Simple but clean. The monastery sunrise is unmissable.

Dingboche 4,410m
Himalayan Dingboche Hotel / Eco Home Lodge

Both have heating in dining rooms — essential at 4,400m in the evening. Good food. Stock up on snacks here before going higher.

Lobuche 4,940m
Eco Lodge Lobuche

Basic rooms, functional kitchen. Lobuche gets very crowded in peak season — arrive by 2pm to secure a room. Few options here.

Gorak Shep 5,140m
Everest Base Camp Lodge / Gorak Shep Lodge

Most expensive and most basic stop on the route ($15–25/night). Very cold. Rooms are small and thin. You'll only spend one night here — it's worth it.

Packing List

Pack light — you'll carry this for 14 days. Everything on this list can be bought or rented in Kathmandu's Thamel district for 30–50% of retail price. Don't buy expensive gear at home — rent in Kathmandu.

Footwear

  • Waterproof trekking boots (broken in before the trek)
  • Gaiters (for snow sections above 4,500m)
  • Camp sandals or Crocs
  • Merino wool hiking socks (5–6 pairs)

Clothing

  • Moisture-wicking base layers (2 sets)
  • Fleece mid-layer
  • Down jacket (rated -10°C minimum)
  • Waterproof shell jacket and trousers
  • Insulated gloves + liner gloves
  • Merino wool beanie, sun hat, buff/neck gaiter

Gear

  • 50–60L backpack with rain cover
  • Trekking poles (essential for descents, saves knees)
  • Sleeping bag liner (teahouse duvets vary in cleanliness)
  • Headlamp + spare batteries
  • Category 4 sunglasses (glacial UV is intense)
  • 20,000mAh power bank

Health

  • Diamox (acetazolamide) — get prescription before leaving
  • Ibuprofen + paracetamol
  • Rehydration salts (ORS)
  • Blister kit (Compeed etc.)
  • Pulse oximeter (~$20, absolutely worth it)
  • Water filter or purification tablets

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does EBC trek cost in 2026?

A standard guided 14-day EBC trek from a reputable Nepali agency costs $1,400–$2,500 per person, including Lukla flights, permits, guide, porter, accommodation and most meals. Budget independent trekkers can manage $950–$1,400 without a guide. Luxury packages with helicopter return run $3,500–$5,500+.

Do I need a guide for EBC in 2026?

Independent trekking is still permitted in the Everest region as of 2026 — unlike many other Nepal routes where solo trekking without a guide is now banned. However, a licensed guide ($25–35/day) is strongly recommended for safety, altitude monitoring and logistics. Most agencies include a guide in their packages.

What is the best time to do EBC trek?

October is the single best month — post-monsoon clear skies, perfect temperatures, stable weather. November is excellent too with fewer crowds. Spring (March–May) is the second best window, with rhododendron blooms and warmer days. Avoid June–September (monsoon) and December–February (extreme cold at high altitude).

How fit do I need to be?

You should be able to hike uphill for 6–8 hours on consecutive days. No climbing experience needed — EBC is a walk, not a climb. Start training 3 months before: long hikes on consecutive days, stair climbing with a weighted pack, good cardio base. The altitude is the main challenge, not the terrain.

Is there WiFi and phone signal?

Yes. Ncell and NTC SIM cards work up to Gorak Shep, though signal becomes patchy above Namche. Most teahouses offer WiFi for $2–15/day depending on altitude. Buy a local SIM card in Kathmandu ($3–5 with data) — it's far cheaper than roaming.

Can I charge electronics on the trail?

Yes, but teahouses charge $1–6 per charge depending on altitude. Bring a 20,000mAh power bank as backup — cold temperatures drain batteries fast. Keep electronics inside your sleeping bag at night above 4,000m.

Is travel insurance mandatory?

Most trekking agencies require it, and you'd be foolish to go without it. Standard travel insurance does NOT cover high-altitude trekking. You need a dedicated adventure policy that specifically covers trekking above 5,000m and helicopter evacuation (which can cost $5,000–$15,000 without coverage). Budget $80–200 for comprehensive cover.

Where is the last ATM?

Namche Bazaar has reliable ATMs — it's the last place you can withdraw cash. Above Namche, there are no ATMs and no card payments. Withdraw all the cash you need for the rest of the trek in Namche. Budget around $50–70/day for food, extras, and tips.
